# Category-management principles

The reasoning a category review rests on. Each entry gives the principle,
what to check in the data, and the common mistake.

## Core principles

### 1. Know what the category is for
Every category has a role: **destination** (the reason shoppers choose the
store), **routine** (bought regularly, price-checked), **occasional or
seasonal** (bought at certain times), or **convenience** (completes the trip,
little price scrutiny). The role decides how much range, space, stock, price
investment and promotion the category earns, and which targets it is judged on.
- **Check:** penetration, purchase frequency, share of store sales and margin,
  seasonality, how much is sold on promotion.
- **Mistake:** labelling a category a destination because it sells a lot. It
  is only a destination if the store does something there that competitors do
  not. The role is the buyer's call; data can suggest it, not settle it. Some
  categories change role through the year.

### 2. Separate a demand problem from an execution problem
When sales miss, first ask whether the product was on the shelf, at the right
price, in the planned position. Weak sales with poor availability is
execution; weak sales with full availability is demand. Only then break the
demand change into its parts: fewer buyers, the same buyers coming less often,
smaller baskets, or a competitor taking share.
- **Check:** in-stock rate, price charged vs price intended, whether a reset or
  markdown actually happened on the planned date.
- **Mistake:** cutting a line for low sales when it was out of stock half the
  period. Sales in a stocked-out period understate demand.

### 3. Follow where lost demand goes
When a line is removed, repriced or unavailable, its buyers split: some buy a
substitute you still carry (often at a different margin), some buy elsewhere,
some buy nothing. Judge the move on what happens to the whole category, not
the moved line.
- **Check:** sales of neighbouring lines during past out-of-stock windows;
  category units and margin before and after.
- **Mistake:** assuming all volume transfers. A stockout recaptures more than a
  permanent delist, because shoppers expect the line back. Removing a whole
  sub-segment loses more than removing scattered lines, because the second
  choices go too.

### 4. Measure return on the stock, not margin alone
GMROI (gross margin dollars per dollar of average stock at cost) combines
margin with how fast the stock turns. Turns is usually the lever a buyer
controls more easily.
- **Check:** GMROI and turns by subcategory and for the lines holding the most
  stock; read the ratio alongside the absolute GM$.
- **Mistake:** comparing a slow structural category with a fast one, or
  leaning on GMROI where the supplier owns most of the stock (it separates
  little there). A high GMROI on a tiny base is still a small contribution.

### 5. Price on cost, competition and value together
Cost-plus ignores what shoppers will pay. Matching competitors gives your
price to someone else. Value-based pricing forgets that substitutes cap the
ceiling. A price is defensible only when all three agree.
- **Check:** unit cost and contribution, the competitor index, sales at
  different price points.
- **Mistake:** defending a price on one lens alone. For a true commodity there
  is little to decide: the market sets the price.

### 6. Count only costs that change, and treat sunk stock differently
Price to maximise contribution (unit margin × units), counting only costs that
move with the decision. Once stock cannot be reordered or returned (end of
season, discontinued, limited run), the cost is already spent: price to
recover the most revenue.
- **Check:** reorderable or returnable flag, lifecycle status, sell-through
  against the time left.
- **Mistake:** refusing to clear dead stock below cost, or applying clearance
  logic to a line you will keep reordering.

### 7. In heavily promoted categories, promoted sales are real demand
Where a category is on promotion most weeks, promoted sales carry the demand
signal and should not be discounted. Shoppers there are loyal to the category
but flexible on the product. Elsewhere, some promoted volume is new and some is
switched from a dearer line or pulled forward from later weeks.
- **Check:** base-to-promoted ratio, the dip after a promotion, movement from
  large to small packs during the event.
- **Mistake:** treating all promoted volume as incremental, or all of it as
  noise.

### 8. A model's answer supports the decision; it does not make it
Optimisation outputs are usually single-category and blind to the basket,
promotions, shelf position and competitor reaction. A move that wins the
category can lose a linked category or a destination trip.
- **Check:** whether the line drives trips or sits in many baskets.
- **Mistake:** quoting a modelled uplift as a point estimate. Give a range,
  name the assumptions, keep a person signing off on image and positioning,
  and revisit through the season.

## Strategy principles

### 9. Strategy comes first
Each category decision should support what the store stands for: price
leader, mainstream, premium or specialist. A move that wins one category but
contradicts the store's promise is the wrong move.
- **Check:** whether price index, private-label share, premium mix and range
  breadth match the stated positioning.
- **Mistake:** judging every category against one house-wide scorecard, or
  letting one strong number override the fit with the store.

### 10. Own the category plan
A supplier's proposal is pitched to every retailer and optimises that
supplier's volume. Accept it only where the whole category and the store also
gain, and keep an independent read of the data.
- **Check:** total category units and GM$ before and after, not the featured
  brand's uplift; cannibalisation of other lines.
- **Mistake:** running the lead supplier's plan unchanged. Supplier expertise
  is useful input; the decision stays with the retailer.

## Supporting rules

- **Cut on substitutability, not sales rank.** A safe cut is a low-loyalty
  line whose buyers switch to something you keep. An expensive cut is a
  slow line that is the only option for a need or a loyal group.
- **Put price where it is noticed.** Shoppers judge value on a small set of
  frequently bought, well-known items. Price those sharply and hold margin on
  the tail, where no one compares.
- **Track competitors without following them.** Hold a sensible position
  against the market; treat the index as a constraint, not the goal.
- **Review as often as the category changes.** Fast-moving or high-value
  categories need frequent reviews; slow, small ones less. Reset targets at
  each review rather than checking against stale ones.
- **Manage the realised price.** What matters is the price actually
  transacted after promotions, markdowns and rebates, not the shelf ticket.
